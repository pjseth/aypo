Product design · wheat procurement · United Flour Mill, Thailand

Grain Desk

A single source of truth for a flour mill's wheat buying — built around the one number that decides it: what this cargo costs against what everyone else paid.

Design proposal, not a build Source: 71-minute walkthrough with Pavin Sethbhakdi, 4 Oct 2026 Timestamps cite the recording

Why the units matter

Every number has to be legible in dollars per cargo

One cargo55,000 tUS wheat, Supramax with gear, PNW to Koh Sichang
At ~$350/t$19.25MFinanced per vessel, 9–10 vessels a year
A $5/t move$275,000"It can increase like that within a few days"
Wheat as share of mill cost80%Procurement is most of the income statement

Pavin does not think in dollars per tonne. He converts, every time, into what a move costs across a 55,000-tonne cargo — and then into the baht-per-kilo flour price he has to quote a customer next quarter. A product that shows $358/t and stops there is making him do the arithmetic that matters.

Design rule: no price appears in one unit. Always $/t, $/cargo, and where the chain reaches that far, ฿/kg of flour.

The one that should worry us

The Mitsui email at 20:14 quotes five classes. The spreadsheet rows it produced captured four — NS14.0 = USD373.80 was dropped. His existing automation already loses data silently, and he has no way to know. That is the strongest argument for an intake agent that reports what it could not read instead of quietly writing a row.

The shape of the problem

One loop, broken in six places

Procurement at UFM is a single loop that runs about ten times a year. Each break in it is a place where Pavin currently substitutes his own attention for a system.

Needs he named

What he asked for, and what answers it

Seven needs came directly from Pavin. Two more we are proposing, marked as ours — he did not ask for them, and they should be presented to him as proposals rather than requirements. All nine are delivered by three agents, set out in the next section.

The number that sets our price

On his own figures, the consultancy's success fee is $0.50/t × 55,000 t ≈ $27,500 a cargo — call it $275,000 a year across ten cargoes, against an $18,000 annual retainer. The Buyer is not competing with $1,500 a month. It is competing with a quarter of a million dollars a year in success fees, which is also the ceiling on what this product can credibly charge.

The platform

Three agents, one desk state

An earlier draft of this document proposed ten agents. That was a list of capabilities wearing costumes — ten things to build, ten things to explain, and no obvious answer to "which one do I open?" Three is the real number, because there are only three questions Pavin asks in a working week.

Each agent is specified the same way: a trigger, typed inputs, a typed output, a confidence it is willing to state, and a named human who approves anything that leaves the building. They write into one shared desk state and the surfaces render it. Nothing contacts a supplier on its own.

What should I pay?

The Buyer

Turns a day of unstructured indications into one defensible counter-offer.

Reads each indication into structured rows — one per class, chained onto any quote it corrects or recalls — and names the fields it could not read rather than guessing. Rebuilds futures, basis and freight from the market so the flat price can be taken apart, and reports the residual nobody itemised. Sets that against what the other eight approved suppliers are showing, what this supplier has conceded before, and what the other Thai mills appear to have paid. Then drafts the counter and the argument for it.

Trigger
On message arrival; on request before a counter
Emits
quote[] · unread_fields[] · components{futures,basis,freight} · residual_margin · peer_basis_est · counter_price · rationale[] · saved_$/t
Approval
Drafts only. Pavin sends.

Folds in Quote Clerk · Fair Value · Negotiator · Scout

When should I move?

The Watch

Owns the clock — the release, the daily picture, and the position carried against both.

Its sharpest mode is the one that wins the demo: at a WASDE or export-sales release it holds a pre-release expectation sheet, diffs the print against it within seconds, scores each class, prices the move against his actual open position and wakes him only if the surprise clears a threshold he set. The rest of the week it writes the morning brief on his own driver taxonomy, and keeps the position book — the 70/30 flat and basis split, the contract legs with Open and Filled status, mark-to-market, breakeven, and the take-profit and loss-cut levels he already sets by hand.

Trigger
Release times, to the second; daily 06:00 ICT; on fill; at settlement
Emits
verdict/class · surprise_vs_expected · $/t · $/cargo · driver[]{stance,confidence,$/t} · legs[] · mtm · breakeven · target_breach
Approval
Alerts freely. Orders never — it tells him to call Mitsui.

Folds in Night Watch · The Analyst · Position Keeper

Did I get what I bought?

The Clerk

Follows the cargo from bill of lading to the baht-per-kilo it becomes.

Splits the 45-page vessel pack into its certificates, classifies each page, and reconciles what the paperwork says against what the contract said — per-hold weights against the bill of lading, protein and falling number against the GAFTA spec — raising a claim when they disagree and flagging missing documents before the vessel berths. Then carries the landed cost through the blend: the cheapest mix of the five classes that still meets each flour grade's spec, priced through to ฿/kg per SKU and the margin on each customer's quarterly offer.

Trigger
On document pack; on ETA countdown; on price move
Emits
page_class[] · completeness · spec_variance · claim_candidate · blend{class:%} · cost_฿/kg · margin_by_SKU
Approval
Raises claims for review. Never files one.

The tenth one was never an agent

"Desk Memory" — ask anything across every quote, message, document and decision — does not become a fourth agent, and it does not belong inside any of the three. It is a property of the shared record: if all three agents write sourced, timestamped output into one store, asking questions of it is a query, not a colleague. Build the store properly and the feature is free. Build three separate agent memories and no amount of work buys it back.

That is the test for anything else proposed later: if it needs its own trigger and its own judgement, it is an agent. If it only needs to read what the three already wrote, it is a surface.

Non-negotiable

Every agent output carries its provenance and is allowed to abstain. Nobody commits $19M on an unsourced number, and a parser that silently guesses is worse than one that says what it could not read — we have the dropped NS14.0 row to prove it.

Surface 1 — the interrupt

Night Watch

This is not a page. It is an interrupt, designed for a man who is half asleep at midnight and has about five minutes of advantage to spend. It must be readable in one glance, state the consequence in money, and offer exactly one action.

I literally wait until midnight on these days — or 2am, 1am if there's daylight savings. As soon as it comes out, it's like waiting for your SAT score. You have to fat finger, be very quick to click on this. "Oh, the file's not available yet." … You scroll down to like page 11 or 12, and then — bam. You quickly call Mitsui: if you're in the market right now, buy me this, sell these futures. Pavin, 41:10 – 42:30

Four things in that passage are specification, not colour. The release lands at midnight or 2am Bangkok depending on US daylight saving, so the schedule is not a fixed local time. He is manually refreshing a page for a PDF that is not there yet. The wheat numbers he needs are on page 11 or 12 of a long document. And he already knows the answer:

You have to have a thing that can instantly — an API that can pull this report out. Pavin, 42:40

Surface 2 — the daily surface

Quote Board and the decomposition

Ten or more indications a day arrive as flat numbers in prose. The board's job is to make them comparable, and the decomposition's job is to show what is actually inside the number — including the part the supplier did not itemise.

Surface 3 — the position

Position Book

This one we do not get to invent: his workbook already defines it. The design job is to keep his model intact — the flat/basis split, the legs, the thresholds — and add only what Excel cannot do, which is tell him when something has moved.

Surfaces 4–8, in brief

The rest of the desk

Morning Brief — the landing surface

One screen at 07:00 ICT: what moved overnight, which drivers changed stance, what is awaiting his approval, and the one decision today actually requires. Built on his own causal taxonomy — weather, crop condition, production, supply, seasonal, export, demand, Commitment of Traders, government policy, forex — with a bearish/bullish split he already reads daily from his brokers, and a $/t sensitivity on each lever.

Cargo Pipeline

Vessels on the water, each with laycan, discharge port, the co-load partners sharing the hull, and a document completeness meter driven by The Clerk's page classification. Per-hold weights reconciled against the bill of lading; quality certificates against the GAFTA spec. Despatch and demurrage exposure sits here too — it appears in his OneNote and in nobody's dashboard.

Benchmark & Savings Ledger

Two things on one page because they answer the same question: did we buy well? The benchmark estimates peer laid-in cost with an honest confidence band. The ledger attributes every $/t saved to the action that produced it — a counter accepted, a basis locked on a dip, a freight line challenged. That ledger is what justifies the subscription at renewal.

Blend & margin bridge · Ask the Desk

The bridge carries the purchase through to ฿/kg per flour grade and the margin on each customer's quarterly offer, closing the loop to the workbook at 17:52. Ask the Desk is plain retrieval over everything the desk has ever held — the answer to a head of procurement who keeps it in his head.

The risk Pavin raised himself

There might have to be a lot of room for tailoring it to what a company needs — like how Salesforce or SAP would tailor the ERP system to a company based on its needs. They might have their own way of doing things… getting them to adopt the app you guys would make, it has to be very easy, and in their own way that they're comfortable with. Pavin, 64:10

Our mitigation is that everything specific to a desk — origins, classes, suppliers, instruments, discharge ports, currency, contract standard — lives in a desk profile, not in code. The test of whether that worked is not UFM. It is the second mill, and whether it costs a configuration file or a rewrite.

Note the second half of his warning, which is about adoption rather than architecture: the product has to work the way he already works. That is the reason the Position Book reproduces his column names instead of improving them.

Where we disagree with him, and why it is worth raising

Asked what to build first, Pavin said: "the first thing that's easiest to make is the fragmented part, putting things together. The research thing is interesting and that'll be harder." [65:30]

He is right about difficulty and we are sequencing against him anyway — Night Watch first, consolidation second. The reason is that consolidation is the thing he has already built for himself, so rebuilding it proves we can match his spreadsheet; it does not prove we can do something he cannot. Night Watch does, it is standalone, and it lands on a night he is already awake. This is a disagreement to put to him directly rather than resolve in a document — he is the one who offered to be a design partner.

Before building

What we still need to ask him

  1. The Competitor Prices tab exists — what is in it, and where do those numbers come from? This decides whether N7 is tractable.
  2. Is the 70/30 flat/basis split policy, per-cargo negotiation, or his own judgement? It changes whether the Position Book proposes splits or just records them.
  3. Who else touches PROCUREMENT TRACKER — is it private to him, or shared with Khun Pracha? That determines whether we are replacing a personal tool or a team one, which is a different product.
  4. What does AVS actually deliver weekly, and what does the $1,500 buy that his daily broker decks and the US Wheat letters do not?
  5. Does the Power Automate flow write only to wheat_price_template, or also into the tracker's Price Indications tab?
  6. Who owns the mailbox decision — and would he pay for the desk, or only for the brokering that comes after it? He deflected this once already.

Two of these are easy to ask, because he volunteered the channel himself: "You can be a design partner if you'd like… if you have any questions, just schedule a meeting with me." [66:00]

He also gave us one piece of homework, and it should be done before any of the above: "Check first to see if there's any tools out there. I doubt it. And maybe that's also — maybe it's because it's too complicated, and that's why there's no tools." [63:40] That second sentence is the real question. If no one has built this, the interesting possibility is not that nobody noticed; it is that the per-customer tailoring makes it uneconomic. We should find out which before committing.
